Traditional option pricing models rely on point estimates of parameters, ignoring the uncertainty inherent in parameter estimation. For agricultural commodities — where prices exhibit mean reversion, jumps, and stochastic volatility — this uncertainty is substantial. A small change in estimated volatility can shift an option value by tens of percent, yet classical methods provide no measure of that sensitivity. This paper develops a Bayesian MCMC framework that estimates three stochastic processes and propagates the full posterior uncertainty into real option values.

We identify three research gaps at the intersection of commodity modeling and real options analysis. Gap 1 — Choice of stochastic process: Which model best captures commodity price dynamics — Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM), mean-reverting jump-diffusion (MRJ), or mean reversion with stochastic volatility (MRS)? Gap 2 — Volatility estimation: How should parameter uncertainty from volatility estimation propagate to option values, rather than collapsing into a single plug-in number? Gap 3 — Bayesian–real options intersection: What happens when we use full posterior distributions instead of point estimates? Our framework answers all three by delivering complete distributions of option values — not point estimates — enabling decision-makers to assess risk across the entire parameter space.

TipLearn more: Why agricultural commodities?

Agricultural commodities sit at the nexus of food security, global trade, and financial markets. Price volatility in corn, soybeans, wheat, live cattle, and soybean meal directly affects farmers’ planting decisions, processors’ hedging strategies, and governments’ food-security policies. Unlike equities, commodity prices are driven by weather shocks, crop cycles, storage costs, and geopolitical disruptions — all of which create the non-Gaussian, fat-tailed dynamics that standard models struggle to capture. Understanding and pricing the optionality embedded in commodity investments (when to plant, harvest, store, or abandon) is therefore both economically significant and methodologically challenging.

TipLearn more: What are Real Options?

A real option is the right — but not the obligation — to make an investment decision about a real (non-financial) asset. Just as a financial call option gives you the right to buy a stock at a preset price, a timing option gives a farmer or trader the right to wait before investing, capturing upside if prices rise. An abandonment option is the analogous put: the right to walk away from a project if prices fall below a threshold, limiting downside losses.

Real options theory recognizes that flexibility has value. The ability to delay, expand, contract, or abandon a project in response to new information is worth something — and that value is missed by traditional net-present-value (NPV) analysis, which assumes a now-or-never decision. In this paper, we value timing and abandonment options for five CBOT-traded agricultural commodities.

TipLearn more: Why Bayesian?

The frequentist approach produces a single point estimate of each model parameter (e.g., volatility \(\sigma = 0.25\)). Decisions based on that number ignore the fact that the estimate is uncertain — it could plausibly be 0.20 or 0.30, and the resulting option value would differ substantially.

The Bayesian approach, by contrast, produces a posterior distribution for each parameter. Instead of one \(\sigma\), you get thousands of plausible \(\sigma\) values drawn via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). When you simulate option payoffs using each draw, you obtain a distribution of option values — complete with credible intervals, tail risks, and probability statements. This gives decision-makers a full picture of the uncertainty they face, rather than a false sense of precision.

TipLearn more: What are log-returns?

The log-return (or continuously compounded return) is defined as

\[r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)\]

where \(P_t\) is the price at time \(t\). Log-returns are preferred over simple returns \((P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}\) for two key reasons:

  1. Time additivity. Multi-period log-returns are the sum of single-period log-returns: \(r_{[t,t+k]} = r_t + r_{t+1} + \cdots + r_{t+k}\). This makes aggregation across weeks trivial.
  2. Symmetry. A +5% log-return followed by a –5% log-return returns you to exactly the original price. Simple returns do not have this property.

For small values, log-returns and simple returns are nearly identical, but the mathematical convenience of log-returns is indispensable in stochastic calculus and time-series modeling.

TipLearn more: Why test for normality?

The Jarque-Bera (JB) test jointly tests whether a distribution’s skewness equals zero and its kurtosis equals three (the normal-distribution values). Under the null hypothesis of normality:

\[JB = \frac{n}{6}\left(S^2 + \frac{(K-3)^2}{4}\right) \sim \chi^2(2)\]

For all five commodities in our sample, the JB statistic exceeds critical values by orders of magnitude (all \(p < 0.001\)), decisively rejecting normality. This means the standard Black-Scholes assumption of log-normal prices fails — returns are fatter-tailed and more skewed than a Gaussian distribution predicts. This motivates the use of flexible stochastic models (MRJ with jumps, MRS with stochastic volatility) that can capture these features.

TipLearn more: What does the ADF test measure?

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test tests the null hypothesis that a time series contains a unit root — i.e., it is non-stationary, with shocks having permanent effects. If the ADF test statistic is sufficiently negative, we reject the null and conclude the series is stationary.

For our commodity log-returns, ADF statistics range from –8.8 to –12.1, all well below the 1% critical value of approximately –3.43. This confirms that log-returns are stationary, even though price levels are not. Stationary returns are a prerequisite for estimating mean-reverting stochastic processes (such as the Schwartz Model 1 used in our MRJ specification) and for standard MCMC inference to produce valid posterior distributions.

TipLearn more: Fat tails and skewness

Kurtosis measures how heavy the tails of a distribution are relative to the normal distribution, which has kurtosis = 3. Values above 3 indicate leptokurtosis (fat tails): extreme price moves occur more frequently than a Gaussian model would predict. In our data, kurtosis ranges from 5.7 (Soybean Meal) to 13.4 (Wheat), meaning extreme weekly swings are far more common than normal theory suggests.

Skewness measures asymmetry. Four of our five commodities display negative skewness (ranging from –0.51 to –0.78), indicating a longer left tail — large drops are more common than large gains. Wheat is the exception with positive skew (0.91), driven by occasional price spikes from supply disruptions.

For option pricing, both features matter:

  • Fat tails increase the probability of extreme payoffs, making options worth more than Black-Scholes implies.
  • Negative skewness makes put options (e.g., abandonment options) particularly valuable, as large downside moves are more likely than a symmetric model predicts.

These stylized facts underpin our choice to go beyond GBM and include jump-diffusion and stochastic-volatility specifications.

2 Stochastic Models

Three stochastic differential equations (SDEs) capture different aspects of commodity price dynamics. Each model makes different assumptions about drift, mean reversion, jumps, and volatility.

2.1 GBM — Geometric Brownian Motion

\[dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\]

Intuition: Random walk with drift. No memory, no gravity. Prices can wander anywhere.

Parameter Meaning
\(\mu\) Drift (annualized expected return)
\(\sigma\) Volatility (annualized standard deviation)

2.2 MRJ — Mean-Reverting Jump-Diffusion

\[d\ln S = \kappa(\theta - \ln S)\,dt + \sigma\,dW + J\,dN\]

Intuition: Pulled back to equilibrium, with occasional shocks. Prices revert to a long-run level but can jump suddenly.

Parameter Meaning
\(\kappa\) Reversion speed
\(\theta\) Long-run equilibrium (log-price)
\(\sigma\) Diffusion volatility
\(\lambda\) Jump intensity (jumps per year)
\(\mu_J\) Mean jump size
\(\sigma_J\) Jump size std. deviation

2.3 MRS — Mean-Reverting + Stochastic Volatility

\[d\ln S = \kappa(\theta - \ln S)\,dt + e^{h_t/2}\,dW_1\]

\[dh_t = \alpha(\beta - h_t)\,dt + \xi\,dW_2, \quad \text{Corr}(dW_1, dW_2) = \rho\]

Intuition: Mean reversion with breathing volatility. The market’s nervousness itself fluctuates over time.

Parameter Meaning
\(\kappa\) Price reversion speed
\(\theta\) Price equilibrium (log-price)
\(\alpha\) Volatility reversion speed
\(\beta\) Long-run log-variance
\(\xi\) Vol-of-vol
\(\rho\) Price-vol correlation (leverage)

function randn() {
  let u = 0, v = 0;
  while (u === 0) u = Math.random();
  while (v === 0) v = Math.random();
  return Math.sqrt(-2.0 * Math.log(u)) * Math.cos(2.0 * Math.PI * v);
}

  const S0 = p.S0;
  const nPaths = 50, nWeeks = 52, dt = 1 / 52;
  const paths = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < nPaths; i++) {
    let logS = Math.log(S0);
    paths.push({week: 0, price: S0, path: i});
    for (let t = 1; t <= nWeeks; t++) {
      logS += (mu - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * dt + sigma * Math.sqrt(dt) * randn();
      paths.push({week: t, price: Math.exp(logS), path: i});
    }
  }
  return paths;
}

simMRJ = {
  simTrigger;
  const key = commodityKey(simCommodity);
  const p = simParams[key];
  const {kappa, theta, sigma, lambda, mu_j, sigma_j} = p.mrj;
  const S0 = p.S0;
  const nPaths = 50, nWeeks = 52, dt = 1 / 52;
  const paths = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < nPaths; i++) {
    let logS = Math.log(S0);
    paths.push({week: 0, price: S0, path: i});
    for (let t = 1; t <= nWeeks; t++) {
      // Mean-reversion in log-space
      logS += kappa * (theta - logS) * dt + sigma * Math.sqrt(dt) * randn();
      // Poisson jump (probability lambda*dt per step)
      if (Math.random() < lambda * dt) {
        logS += mu_j + sigma_j * randn();
      }
      paths.push({week: t, price: Math.exp(logS), path: i});
    }
  }
  return paths;
}

simMRS = {
  simTrigger;
  const key = commodityKey(simCommodity);
  const p = simParams[key];
  const {kappa, theta, alpha, beta, xi, rho} = p.mrs;
  const S0 = p.S0;
  const nPaths = 50, nWeeks = 52, dt = 1 / 52;
  const paths = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < nPaths; i++) {
    let logS = Math.log(S0);
    let h = beta;  // initialize log-variance at long-run level
    paths.push({week: 0, price: S0, path: i});
    for (let t = 1; t <= nWeeks; t++) {
      const eps1 = randn();
      // Correlated noise via Cholesky decomposition
      const eps2 = rho * eps1 + Math.sqrt(1 - rho * rho) * randn();
      const volNow = Math.exp(h / 2);
      // Price equation
      logS += kappa * (theta - logS) * dt + volNow * Math.sqrt(dt) * eps1;
      // Log-variance equation
      h += alpha * (beta - h) * dt + xi * Math.sqrt(dt) * eps2;
      paths.push({week: t, price: Math.exp(logS), path: i});
    }
  }
  return paths;
}

TipLearn more: SDE derivations

GBM — from SDE to log-normal solution

Starting from \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\), apply Ito’s lemma to \(f(S) = \ln S\):

\[d\ln S = \left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW\]

Integrating from \(0\) to \(T\):

\[\ln S_T = \ln S_0 + \left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T + \sigma W_T\]

Since \(W_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, T)\), \(\ln S_T\) is normally distributed, so \(S_T\) is log-normal. This is the exact solution — no discretization needed.

MRJ — The log-price follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process with superimposed compound Poisson jumps. No closed-form solution exists for the transition density, so simulation uses Euler-Maruyama discretization with jumps drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\) at Poisson-random times.

MRS — This is a two-dimensional system (log-price + log-variance). Both components are simulated via Euler-Maruyama. The correlation between the two Brownian motions is induced through Cholesky decomposition: \(\varepsilon_2 = \rho\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\varepsilon_2^*\) where \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2^* \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) are independent.

TipLearn more: Why GBM fails for commodities

GBM assumes prices follow a random walk with drift — there is no tendency to return to any particular level. For financial assets like stocks, this can be reasonable. For commodities, three features make GBM inadequate:

  1. No mean reversion. Commodity prices are anchored by production costs and storage economics. When prices are far above marginal cost, supply increases and prices fall; when prices are below cost, supply contracts. GBM misses this gravitational pull entirely.

  2. Prices can drift to infinity (or zero). Under GBM, the expected price grows exponentially: \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0 e^{\mu T}\). In 20 years of corn data, we see prices oscillating in a range, not trending to infinity.

  3. The Samuelson effect. Futures price volatility increases as the contract approaches maturity — a pattern documented by Samuelson (1965). GBM has constant volatility by construction.

Additionally, commodities have storage costs and convenience yields that create a term structure absent from simple GBM. The Schwartz (1997) one-factor model explicitly incorporates mean reversion to address these issues.

TipLearn more: What are Poisson jumps?

In the MRJ model, the term \(J\,dN\) represents jump risk: discrete, sudden price movements that cannot be captured by continuous diffusion alone.

\(dN\) is a Poisson counting process with intensity \(\lambda\) (jumps per year). In any small interval \(dt\):

  • Probability of one jump: \(\lambda\,dt\)
  • Probability of no jump: \(1 - \lambda\,dt\)
  • Jump size \(J \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\) — drawn from a normal distribution

For our commodities, estimated \(\lambda\) values range from 0.03 (Wheat — rare but large jumps) to 0.23 (Corn — more frequent smaller jumps). In simulation, each weekly time step has probability \(\lambda / 52\) of experiencing a jump.

Real-world examples of commodity jumps:

  • 2012 US Drought: Corn prices surged 60% in 8 weeks
  • 2020 COVID lockdowns: Live Cattle dropped 30% in 3 weeks
  • 2022 Ukraine invasion: Wheat spiked 70% in 2 weeks
  • Frost events in Brazil: Coffee can jump 20-30% overnight

These discrete shocks are fundamentally different from the smooth, continuous price changes that GBM assumes. Jump-diffusion models capture both the “normal” and “extreme” components of price dynamics.

TipLearn more: Stochastic volatility

In financial markets, volatility is not constant — it clusters. Periods of high volatility tend to be followed by more high volatility, and calm periods tend to persist. This phenomenon, first documented by Mandelbrot (1963), is visible in any commodity return series.

ARCH/GARCH effects: The ARCH test (Engle, 1982) and GARCH models (Bollerslev, 1986) capture conditional heteroskedasticity — the variance of returns depends on past returns. All five commodities in our study show significant ARCH effects.

The MRS approach: Rather than modeling volatility as a function of past returns (backward-looking), the MRS model lets volatility follow its own mean-reverting stochastic process:

\[dh_t = \alpha(\beta - h_t)\,dt + \xi\,dW_2\]

where \(h_t\) is log-variance. This is forward-looking and allows volatility to evolve independently of returns (modulated by correlation \(\rho\)).

Key parameters:

  • \(\alpha\) (vol reversion speed): How quickly volatility returns to “normal.” Higher \(\alpha\) means volatility spikes are short-lived.
  • \(\beta\) (long-run log-variance): The “normal” level of volatility. \(e^{\beta/2}\) gives the steady-state annualized volatility.
  • \(\xi\) (vol-of-vol): How much volatility itself fluctuates. Higher \(\xi\) means more volatile volatility — wider uncertainty bands.
  • \(\rho\) (leverage): Correlation between price shocks and volatility shocks. For equities, \(\rho < 0\) (prices drop → vol rises). For commodities, \(\rho\) is often near zero.
TipLearn more: The Schwartz (1997) one-factor model

Our MRJ specification is based on Schwartz’s (1997) seminal “Model 1” for commodity pricing. Eduardo Schwartz proposed three increasingly complex models for commodity futures prices; the one-factor model has become a benchmark in the literature.

The core idea: The logarithm of the commodity spot price follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process:

\[d\ln S = \kappa(\theta - \ln S)\,dt + \sigma\,dW\]

This is the continuous-time analogue of a first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) process. The parameter \(\kappa\) controls the speed of mean reversion: how strongly prices are pulled back to the long-run equilibrium level \(e^\theta\).

Half-life of shocks: A useful interpretation is the half-life \(t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/\kappa\). For corn (\(\kappa = 0.24\)), shocks take about 2.9 years to decay by half. For wheat (\(\kappa = 0.73\)), the half-life is only 0.95 years — wheat prices revert much faster.

Our extension: We add compound Poisson jumps to Schwartz’s base model, yielding the MRJ specification. This captures both the gravitational pull toward equilibrium and the sudden supply/demand shocks characteristic of agricultural markets. The jump component was not in Schwartz’s original formulation but has been incorporated by subsequent authors (e.g., Hilliard and Reis, 1998).

Why it matters for real options: Mean reversion fundamentally changes option values compared to GBM. Under mean reversion, long-dated options are worth less than GBM predicts (because extreme prices are pulled back), while short-dated options during periods of price dislocation may be worth more (because the reversion creates predictable value).

Instead of computing a single “best” parameter estimate, Bayesian inference via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) produces a full posterior distribution for each parameter. This captures the uncertainty in our estimates — uncertainty that classical methods hide.

We used Stan’s Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC/NUTS) sampler with 2 chains x 1,500 post-warmup iterations per model-commodity pair, yielding 1,500 posterior draws for each parameter.

TipLearn more: MLE vs Bayesian

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) finds the single parameter value that maximizes the likelihood function — the peak of the mountain. You get one number per parameter and a standard error.

Bayesian estimation maps the entire landscape. You get a full probability distribution for each parameter, capturing asymmetries, multimodality, and correlations between parameters.

Visual analogy: MLE is like finding the summit of a mountain and reporting its altitude. Bayesian is like producing a topographic map of the entire mountain range. With MLE, you know the peak; with Bayesian, you know the terrain — where the ridges run, where the valleys dip, and how uncertain you should be about the summit’s exact location.

For real options, this distinction is critical: a single MLE estimate gives you a single option value, while Bayesian posteriors give you a distribution of option values that honestly reflects parameter uncertainty.

TipLearn more: What is MCMC?

Markov Chain Monte Carlo is a family of algorithms for sampling from probability distributions that are too complex to compute analytically.

The core idea: construct a random walk through parameter space where the probability of visiting any region is proportional to its posterior probability. After enough steps (the “burn-in” or warmup period), the chain converges to the target distribution and subsequent samples are draws from the posterior.

Analogy: Imagine exploring a city by taking random walks. You’ll naturally spend more time in popular, well-connected neighborhoods (high posterior probability) and less time in remote outskirts (low probability). After walking long enough, the fraction of time you spend in each neighborhood reflects its relative popularity.

The “Markov” part means each step depends only on the current position, not the entire history. The “Monte Carlo” part means we use random sampling to approximate a complex calculation.

TipLearn more: HMC and NUTS

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) is an advanced MCMC algorithm that uses gradient information — the slope of the posterior surface — to propose moves. It borrows from physics: imagine a frictionless puck sliding on the posterior surface. The puck naturally rolls toward high-probability regions, exploring the space much more efficiently than a random walk.

NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler) is an extension by Hoffman and Gelman (2014) that automatically determines how far to simulate the trajectory. It stops when the puck would “turn around” — hence “No U-Turn.” This eliminates a finicky tuning parameter (trajectory length) that plagued standard HMC.

Why it matters: Basic Metropolis-Hastings MCMC takes small, random steps and often gets stuck. HMC/NUTS takes long, directed leaps through parameter space, achieving much lower autocorrelation and higher effective sample sizes per iteration. This is why we can get reliable posteriors with just 1,500 iterations.

Stan implements NUTS as its default sampler, and it is widely considered the state-of-the-art for continuous parameter models.

TipLearn more: Prior specification

In Bayesian statistics, priors encode our beliefs about parameters before seeing the data. We used weakly informative priors following recommendations from Gelman et al. (2017):

  • Drift (\(\mu\)): Normal(0, 0.5) — centered at zero, allowing moderate positive or negative drift.
  • Volatility (\(\sigma\)): Half-Normal(0, 1) — positive by construction, broad enough to accommodate both calm and volatile markets.
  • Mean-reversion speed (\(\kappa\)): Half-Normal(0, 2) — weakly constrained to positive values, allowing fast or slow reversion.
  • Jump intensity (\(\lambda\)): Exponential(0.5) — mildly informative, expecting a few jumps per year.

These priors are broad enough to let the data speak (with ~1,000 weekly observations, the likelihood dominates) but constrained enough to keep parameters in physically sensible ranges (e.g., volatility cannot be negative).

TipLearn more: Convergence diagnostics

How do we know MCMC has converged to the true posterior?

  • \(\hat{R}\) (R-hat): Compares the variance within each chain to the variance between chains. If chains have converged to the same distribution, \(\hat{R} \approx 1\). Values below 1.01 are excellent; below 1.05 is acceptable. Values above 1.1 signal a problem.

  • ESS (Effective Sample Size): MCMC samples are autocorrelated — each sample is similar to its neighbors. ESS estimates how many independent samples we effectively have. With 1,500 total draws, ESS > 400 is generally good for reliable posterior summaries.

Rule of thumb: If all parameters have \(\hat{R} < 1.05\) and ESS > 100, the chains have likely converged. Our results show \(\hat{R} < 1.01\) for most parameters, with ESS typically in the hundreds — adequate for the posterior summaries and option valuations we compute.

TipLearn more: Computational budget

Our setup uses 2 chains x 1,500 iterations (after 750 warmup iterations per chain), which we call the “lite” configuration. This is deliberate:

  • Full configuration: 4 chains x 4,000 post-warmup iterations = 16,000 draws. More robust but requires 4-10x more computation time per model.
  • Lite configuration: 2 chains x 750 post-warmup = 1,500 draws. Faster, adequate when diagnostics confirm convergence.

With 15 model-commodity pairs (3 models x 5 commodities), the full setup would require substantial computation. Our convergence diagnostics (\(\hat{R}\) and ESS values shown above) confirm that the lite configuration provides adequate posterior exploration for all fitted models.

When would you need more? Multimodal posteriors, highly correlated parameters, or models with many latent variables (like MRS with ~1,000+ latent volatility states) may benefit from longer chains. For our GBM and MRJ models, 1,500 draws is more than sufficient.

TipLearn more: What is LOO-CV?

Leave-one-out cross-validation estimates predictive accuracy by predicting each data point from the rest. For \(N\) observations, we measure how well the model predicts observation \(i\) when fitted to all observations except \(i\).

Doing this literally would require refitting the model \(N\) times — prohibitively expensive for MCMC. Instead, we use Pareto-Smoothed Importance Sampling (PSIS-LOO), which approximates the leave-one-out predictive densities from a single model fit using importance weights. The result is the LOO-IC (LOO Information Criterion), computed as \(-2 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(y_i | y_{-i})\).

Lower LOO-IC means better out-of-sample predictive performance.

TipLearn more: LOO-IC vs AIC vs BIC

All three are model comparison criteria, but they differ fundamentally:

  • AIC (Akaike) and BIC (Bayesian) are asymptotic approximations based on point estimates. They penalize complexity via the number of parameters.
  • LOO-IC directly estimates out-of-sample predictive accuracy by approximating leave-one-out cross-validation.

For Bayesian models with informative priors or hierarchical structure, AIC and BIC can be misleading because they don’t account for the regularizing effect of priors. LOO-IC uses the full posterior predictive distribution, making it more reliable for our setting. It also handles models with different numbers of effective parameters (like our MRS with ~1,100 latent states) more gracefully than AIC’s simple parameter count.

TipLearn more: Pareto-k diagnostics

Each LOO observation has a Pareto-\(k\) diagnostic that measures how influential that observation is for the posterior. The thresholds are:

Pareto-\(k\) Interpretation
\(k < 0.5\) Good — reliable LOO estimate
\(0.5 \leq k < 0.7\) OK — still usable
\(k \geq 0.7\) Problematic — that observation is very influential

When \(k\) is high, the importance sampling approximation breaks down for that point, and the LOO estimate may be unreliable. Our models mostly have \(k < 0.5\), indicating that PSIS-LOO provides trustworthy model comparisons.

TipLearn more: The MRS caveat

MRS (Mean-Reverting with Stochastic Volatility) has approximately 1,100 latent volatility parameters — one per observation. This gives the model enormous flexibility to fit the data, which is reflected in consistently lower (better) LOO-IC values across all commodities.

The model genuinely captures volatility dynamics that GBM and MRJ miss. However, part of the LOO-IC improvement may come from this flexibility rather than true out-of-sample predictive power. In other words, MRS may be partially “overfitting” to the in-sample volatility pattern.

How to interpret this: MRS is the best model by LOO-IC, but the gap between MRS and MRJ may overstate the true predictive advantage. For real options valuation, we report results for all three models to show how model choice affects option values.

TipLearn more: WAIC as alternative

The Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) is another Bayesian model comparison metric. Like LOO-IC, it estimates out-of-sample predictive accuracy, but it does so by computing the log pointwise predictive density and subtracting a penalty for effective number of parameters.

In our analysis, WAIC and LOO-IC agree completely on model rankings: MRS > MRJ > GBM for all five commodities. This concordance is reassuring — the relative model ordering is robust to the choice of information criterion.

When they disagree (which can happen with highly influential observations), LOO-IC with Pareto-\(k\) diagnostics is generally preferred because it flags problematic observations explicitly.

WarningImportant: Physical vs Risk-Neutral Pricing

The Black-Scholes estimate uses risk-neutral (Q) pricing while the Bayesian estimates use the physical (P) measure. This comparison illustrates the impact of parametric uncertainty on option valuation, not arbitrage-free pricing differences. For a detailed discussion, see Section 5 of the paper.

TipLearn more: What is a timing option?

The right (but not obligation) to delay an investment until conditions improve. Analogy: a farmer deciding WHEN to plant — the flexibility to wait has value. Strike = 110% of current price (\(K = 1.10 \times S_0\)). The option captures how much the ability to wait and observe price movements is worth before committing capital.

TipLearn more: What is an abandonment option?

The right to walk away from a project and recover salvage value. Analogy: a farmer deciding to stop growing a crop and sell the land. Strike = 80% of current price (\(K = 0.80 \times S_0\)). It is insurance against catastrophic loss — if the commodity price drops far enough, the option to exit and recover partial value becomes valuable.

TipLearn more: Strike price rationale

Timing option \(K = 1.10 \times S_0\) means investing when price is 10% above current level. Abandonment \(K = 0.80 \times S_0\) means salvaging at 80% of current price. These are representative thresholds used in the real options literature, not market-traded strike prices. They capture the intuition that timing requires a meaningful upside, and abandonment occurs when losses become substantial.

TipLearn more: Physical measure P vs risk-neutral Q

Black-Scholes uses the risk-neutral measure Q (replacing drift with the risk-free rate). Our Bayesian approach uses the physical measure P (actual market dynamics). Under P, we capture real-world uncertainty including the actual drift and volatility that commodity markets exhibit. This is appropriate for decision-making under uncertainty but cannot directly price traded derivatives. The comparison between BS and Bayesian values highlights how parametric uncertainty — not the measure change — widens the valuation range.

TipLearn more: European vs American options

Our options are European-style (exercised only at maturity, \(T = 1\) year). American options allow early exercise at any time and are more valuable because of this additional flexibility. We use European options because they make Monte Carlo computation tractable: we simply simulate paths to \(T\) and evaluate the payoff, rather than solving a backward induction problem at every time step. This is a common simplification in the real options literature.

TipLearn more: Why Bayesian CIs matter

A classical Black-Scholes estimate says “the timing option is worth $53.” Our Bayesian approach says “the timing option is worth $53 on average, but could range from $28 to $93 with 95% probability.” The difference matters because investment decisions depend on the full range of possible outcomes, not just a point estimate. A risk-averse investor might avoid a project whose option value has large downside uncertainty, even if the mean is favorable. Bayesian credible intervals make this risk visible.

6 Key Takeaways

🔍

6.0.1 Uncertainty Matters

Bayesian credible intervals are 2–5x wider than classical Black-Scholes point estimates. Investment decisions based on single numbers systematically understate risk.

⚖

6.0.2 Model Choice Matters

GBM, MRJ, and MRS produce materially different option values for the same commodity. The choice of stochastic process is not a technical detail — it directly impacts investment decisions.

🏆

6.0.3 MRS Fits Best

Mean-Reverting Stochastic Volatility (MRS) achieves the best LOO-IC across all five commodities. Stochastic volatility captures dynamics that simpler models miss.

⚠

6.0.4 Classical Methods Understate Risk

Point estimates hide the full picture. A timing option “worth $53” could range from $28 to $93 under Bayesian estimation. Decision-makers deserve this information.

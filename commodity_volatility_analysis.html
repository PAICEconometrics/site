A GARCH Approach to Modeling Time-Varying Volatility

Author

PAIC Econometrics Research Team

Published

February 17, 2026

Executive Summary

This study investigates the volatility dynamics of eight major agricultural commodity futures using Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models. We analyze daily log-returns from 2019 to 2026, characterizing the stylized facts of commodity returns and estimating GARCH(1,1) parameters for each market. Our findings reveal significant volatility clustering, fat-tailed distributions, and persistent conditional heteroskedasticity across all commodities, with important implications for risk management and portfolio construction.

NoteResearch Context

This analysis provides the volatility modeling foundation for our multi-objective portfolio optimization framework. Understanding time-varying risk is essential for dynamic hedging strategies and adaptive portfolio rebalancing in agricultural commodity markets.

1 Introduction

Volatility in agricultural commodity markets has been a subject of intense academic and practitioner interest, particularly since the food price crises of 2007–2008 and the market disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic. Understanding the statistical properties of commodity price fluctuations is fundamental to risk management, derivative pricing, and portfolio allocation (Hull 2018).

The seminal work of Mandelbrot (1963) first documented that financial returns exhibit fat tails and volatility clustering—periods of high volatility tend to be followed by high volatility. These stylized facts, later systematized by Cont (2001), motivate the use of autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) models introduced by Engle (1982) and generalized by Bollerslev (1986).

1.1 Study Objectives

Our analysis aims to:

  1. Characterize return distributions through descriptive statistics including skewness and kurtosis
  2. Test for ARCH effects using the Ljung-Box test on squared returns (Ljung and Box 1978)
  3. Estimate GARCH(1,1) models for each commodity to quantify volatility persistence
  4. Visualize volatility clustering through rolling volatility and conditional variance plots
  5. Compare volatility regimes across commodities to identify diversification opportunities

1.2 Literature Review

The GARCH family of models remains the workhorse for volatility modeling in financial econometrics. Hansen and Lunde (2005) conducted an extensive forecast comparison and found that GARCH(1,1) is remarkably difficult to beat in terms of out-of-sample performance. In the agricultural commodity context, Ram'irez and Fadiga (2003) applied asymmetric-error GARCH models to forecasting commodity prices, while Vivian and Wohar (2012) documented structural breaks in commodity volatility.

Recent literature has focused on the interaction between commodity volatility and macroeconomic uncertainty. Fang and Yin (2025) examined how commodity futures can be used to hedge climate change news, highlighting the evolving nature of volatility drivers in agricultural markets. Demiralay, Gencer, and Brauneis (2025) analyzed stock-commodity correlations and their implications for optimal hedging strategies.

2 Methodology

2.1 Data Description

We analyze daily closing prices for eight agricultural commodity futures obtained from Yahoo Finance, covering the period from January 2019 to the present. The commodities include: Soybean, Corn, Coffee, Sugar, Wheat, Cotton, Cocoa, and Cattle futures.

2.2 Log-Returns

Daily log-returns are computed as:

\[ r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) \]

where \(P_t\) is the closing price at time \(t\).

2.3 GARCH(1,1) Model

The GARCH(1,1) model specifies the conditional variance as:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_t &= \mu + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \quad z_t \sim N(0,1) \\ \sigma_t^2 &= \omega + \alpha \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 \end{aligned} \]

where \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \geq 0\), \(\beta \geq 0\), and \(\alpha + \beta < 1\) ensures covariance stationarity. The sum \(\alpha + \beta\) measures volatility persistence: values close to 1 indicate highly persistent shocks.

2.4 Ljung-Box Test for ARCH Effects

To test for the presence of ARCH effects, we apply the Ljung-Box test to the squared returns:

\[ Q(m) = n(n+2) \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{n-k} \]

where \(\hat{\rho}_k\) is the sample autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) at lag \(k\). Rejection of the null hypothesis (no autocorrelation in squared returns) indicates volatility clustering.

3.4 Ljung-Box Test for ARCH Effects

Code 
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import acorr_ljungbox

print("=" * 75)
print("LJUNG-BOX TEST FOR ARCH EFFECTS (SQUARED RETURNS)")
print("=" * 75)
print(f"{'Commodity':<12} {'LB(10)':<12} {'p-value':<12} {'LB(20)':<12} {'p-value':<12} {'ARCH?'}")
print("-" * 75)

lb_results = {}
for commodity in commodities:
    sq_returns = (returns[commodity].dropna() ** 2)
    lb_test = acorr_ljungbox(sq_returns, lags=[10, 20], return_df=True)
    
    lb10 = lb_test.iloc[0]['lb_stat']
    p10 = lb_test.iloc[0]['lb_pvalue']
    lb20 = lb_test.iloc[1]['lb_stat']
    p20 = lb_test.iloc[1]['lb_pvalue']
    arch_present = "YES" if p10 < 0.05 else "NO"
    
    lb_results[commodity] = {'lb10': lb10, 'p10': p10, 'lb20': lb20, 'p20': p20}
    print(f"{commodity:<12} {lb10:<12.2f} {p10:<12.6f} {lb20:<12.2f} {p20:<12.6f} {arch_present}")

print("\nConclusion: All commodities exhibit significant ARCH effects,")
print("justifying the use of GARCH-family models.")

3.5 GARCH(1,1) Estimation

Code 
from arch import arch_model

print("=" * 85)
print("GARCH(1,1) MODEL ESTIMATION")
print("=" * 85)
print(f"{'Commodity':<12} {'ω (×1e6)':<12} {'α':<10} {'β':<10} {'α+β':<10} {'LogLik':<12} {'AIC':<10}")
print("-" * 85)

garch_results = {}
for commodity in commodities:
    ret = returns[commodity].dropna() * 100  # scale to percentage
    
    model = arch_model(ret, vol='Garch', p=1, q=1, mean='Constant', dist='normal')
    result = model.fit(disp='off')
    
    omega = result.params['omega']
    alpha = result.params['alpha[1]']
    beta = result.params['beta[1]']
    persistence = alpha + beta
    
    garch_results[commodity] = {
        'model': result,
        'omega': omega,
        'alpha': alpha,
        'beta': beta,
        'persistence': persistence,
        'loglik': result.loglikelihood,
        'aic': result.aic,
        'cond_vol': result.conditional_volatility
    }
    
    print(f"{commodity:<12} {omega*1e6:<12.4f} {alpha:<10.4f} {beta:<10.4f} {persistence:<10.4f} {result.loglikelihood:<12.1f} {result.aic:<10.1f}")

print("\nNote: α+β close to 1 indicates high volatility persistence.")
avg_persistence = np.mean([v['persistence'] for v in garch_results.values()])
print(f"Average persistence across commodities: {avg_persistence:.4f}")

5 Discussion

5.1 Stylized Facts Confirmed

Our analysis confirms several well-known stylized facts of financial returns in the agricultural commodity context (Cont 2001):

  1. Fat Tails: All return distributions exhibit excess kurtosis, indicating a higher probability of extreme events than predicted by the normal distribution. This has implications for Value-at-Risk calculations and tail risk management.

  2. Volatility Clustering: The significant ARCH effects and high GARCH persistence parameters confirm that large price movements tend to be followed by large movements (of either sign). This is visually evident in the conditional volatility plots.

  3. Asymmetry: Several commodities show significant skewness, suggesting that negative returns may be more extreme than positive ones, consistent with the leverage effect documented in equity markets.

5.2 Implications for Portfolio Management

The heterogeneous volatility dynamics across commodities create opportunities for portfolio diversification:

  • Counter-cyclical volatility: Some commodities exhibit volatility spikes at different times, suggesting potential for risk reduction through diversification (Markowitz 1952).
  • Regime-dependent allocation: The time-varying nature of conditional volatility supports dynamic allocation strategies that adjust portfolio weights based on current volatility estimates.
  • Risk budgeting: GARCH-based volatility forecasts provide essential inputs for risk parity and risk budgeting portfolio construction approaches.

5.3 Limitations

While GARCH(1,1) provides a robust baseline, several extensions could improve modeling accuracy:

  • Asymmetric models (EGARCH, GJR-GARCH) to capture the leverage effect
  • Heavy-tailed innovations (Student-t, GED) to better fit empirical distributions
  • Multivariate GARCH (DCC-GARCH) to model time-varying correlations
  • Regime-switching GARCH to capture structural breaks in volatility (Vivian and Wohar 2012)

6 Conclusions

This study provides comprehensive evidence of heteroskedastic behavior in agricultural commodity returns using GARCH(1,1) models. Key conclusions include:

  1. All eight agricultural commodities exhibit significant volatility clustering and ARCH effects, validating the GARCH modeling framework.
  2. Volatility persistence is uniformly high across commodities, indicating that shocks to volatility dissipate slowly.
  3. Return distributions are consistently non-normal with fat tails, necessitating robust risk management approaches.
  4. The heterogeneous volatility patterns across commodities support the case for diversified portfolio strategies.

These findings provide essential inputs for the subsequent stages of our research program, including multi-objective portfolio optimization and machine learning-based price forecasting.

## Executive Summary {.unnumbered}

This study investigates the volatility dynamics of eight major agricultural commodity futures using Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models. We analyze daily log-returns from 2019 to 2026, characterizing the stylized facts of commodity returns and estimating GARCH(1,1) parameters for each market. Our findings reveal significant volatility clustering, fat-tailed distributions, and persistent conditional heteroskedasticity across all commodities, with important implications for risk management and portfolio construction.

:::{.callout-note}
## Research Context
This analysis provides the volatility modeling foundation for our multi-objective portfolio optimization framework. Understanding time-varying risk is essential for dynamic hedging strategies and adaptive portfolio rebalancing in agricultural commodity markets.
:::

## Introduction

Volatility in agricultural commodity markets has been a subject of intense academic and practitioner interest, particularly since the food price crises of 2007--2008 and the market disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic. Understanding the statistical properties of commodity price fluctuations is fundamental to risk management, derivative pricing, and portfolio allocation [@hull2018options].

The seminal work of @mandelbrot1963variation first documented that financial returns exhibit *fat tails* and *volatility clustering*---periods of high volatility tend to be followed by high volatility. These stylized facts, later systematized by @cont2001empirical, motivate the use of autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) models introduced by @engle1982autoregressive and generalized by @bollerslev1986generalized.

### Study Objectives

Our analysis aims to:

1. **Characterize return distributions** through descriptive statistics including skewness and kurtosis
2. **Test for ARCH effects** using the Ljung-Box test on squared returns [@ljung1978measure]
3. **Estimate GARCH(1,1) models** for each commodity to quantify volatility persistence
4. **Visualize volatility clustering** through rolling volatility and conditional variance plots
5. **Compare volatility regimes** across commodities to identify diversification opportunities

### Literature Review

The GARCH family of models remains the workhorse for volatility modeling in financial econometrics. @hansen2005forecast conducted an extensive forecast comparison and found that GARCH(1,1) is remarkably difficult to beat in terms of out-of-sample performance. In the agricultural commodity context, @ramirez2003forecasting applied asymmetric-error GARCH models to forecasting commodity prices, while @vivian2012commodity documented structural breaks in commodity volatility.

Recent literature has focused on the interaction between commodity volatility and macroeconomic uncertainty. @fang2025hedging examined how commodity futures can be used to hedge climate change news, highlighting the evolving nature of volatility drivers in agricultural markets. @demiralay2025stock analyzed stock-commodity correlations and their implications for optimal hedging strategies.

## Methodology

### Data Description

We analyze daily closing prices for eight agricultural commodity futures obtained from Yahoo Finance, covering the period from January 2019 to the present. The commodities include: Soybean, Corn, Coffee, Sugar, Wheat, Cotton, Cocoa, and Cattle futures.

### Log-Returns

Daily log-returns are computed as:

$$
r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)
$$

where $P_t$ is the closing price at time $t$.

### GARCH(1,1) Model

The GARCH(1,1) model specifies the conditional variance as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_t &= \mu + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \quad z_t \sim N(0,1) \\
\sigma_t^2 &= \omega + \alpha \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\omega > 0$, $\alpha \geq 0$, $\beta \geq 0$, and $\alpha + \beta < 1$ ensures covariance stationarity. The sum $\alpha + \beta$ measures **volatility persistence**: values close to 1 indicate highly persistent shocks.

### Ljung-Box Test for ARCH Effects

To test for the presence of ARCH effects, we apply the Ljung-Box test to the squared returns:

$$
Q(m) = n(n+2) \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{n-k}
$$

where $\hat{\rho}_k$ is the sample autocorrelation of $r_t^2$ at lag $k$. Rejection of the null hypothesis (no autocorrelation in squared returns) indicates volatility clustering.

### Ljung-Box Test for ARCH Effects

```{python}
#| label: ljung-box-test
#| code-fold: show
#| tbl-cap: "Ljung-Box Test for ARCH Effects (Squared Returns)"

from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import acorr_ljungbox

print("=" * 75)
print("LJUNG-BOX TEST FOR ARCH EFFECTS (SQUARED RETURNS)")
print("=" * 75)
print(f"{'Commodity':<12} {'LB(10)':<12} {'p-value':<12} {'LB(20)':<12} {'p-value':<12} {'ARCH?'}")
print("-" * 75)

lb_results = {}
for commodity in commodities:
    sq_returns = (returns[commodity].dropna() ** 2)
    lb_test = acorr_ljungbox(sq_returns, lags=[10, 20], return_df=True)
    
    lb10 = lb_test.iloc[0]['lb_stat']
    p10 = lb_test.iloc[0]['lb_pvalue']
    lb20 = lb_test.iloc[1]['lb_stat']
    p20 = lb_test.iloc[1]['lb_pvalue']
    arch_present = "YES" if p10 < 0.05 else "NO"
    
    lb_results[commodity] = {'lb10': lb10, 'p10': p10, 'lb20': lb20, 'p20': p20}
    print(f"{commodity:<12} {lb10:<12.2f} {p10:<12.6f} {lb20:<12.2f} {p20:<12.6f} {arch_present}")

print("\nConclusion: All commodities exhibit significant ARCH effects,")
print("justifying the use of GARCH-family models.")
```

### GARCH(1,1) Estimation

```{python}
#| label: garch-estimation
#| code-fold: show
#| tbl-cap: "GARCH(1,1) Parameter Estimates"

from arch import arch_model

print("=" * 85)
print("GARCH(1,1) MODEL ESTIMATION")
print("=" * 85)
print(f"{'Commodity':<12} {'ω (×1e6)':<12} {'α':<10} {'β':<10} {'α+β':<10} {'LogLik':<12} {'AIC':<10}")
print("-" * 85)

garch_results = {}
for commodity in commodities:
    ret = returns[commodity].dropna() * 100  # scale to percentage
    
    model = arch_model(ret, vol='Garch', p=1, q=1, mean='Constant', dist='normal')
    result = model.fit(disp='off')
    
    omega = result.params['omega']
    alpha = result.params['alpha[1]']
    beta = result.params['beta[1]']
    persistence = alpha + beta
    
    garch_results[commodity] = {
        'model': result,
        'omega': omega,
        'alpha': alpha,
        'beta': beta,
        'persistence': persistence,
        'loglik': result.loglikelihood,
        'aic': result.aic,
        'cond_vol': result.conditional_volatility
    }
    
    print(f"{commodity:<12} {omega*1e6:<12.4f} {alpha:<10.4f} {beta:<10.4f} {persistence:<10.4f} {result.loglikelihood:<12.1f} {result.aic:<10.1f}")

print("\nNote: α+β close to 1 indicates high volatility persistence.")
avg_persistence = np.mean([v['persistence'] for v in garch_results.values()])
print(f"Average persistence across commodities: {avg_persistence:.4f}")
```

:::{.callout-important}
## Volatility Persistence
The sum $\alpha + \beta$ measures how quickly volatility shocks decay. Values close to 1 indicate that shocks to volatility persist for extended periods, a hallmark of commodity markets that experience supply disruptions, weather events, and geopolitical shocks [@bollerslev1986generalized].
:::

## Discussion

### Stylized Facts Confirmed

Our analysis confirms several well-known stylized facts of financial returns in the agricultural commodity context [@cont2001empirical]:

1. **Fat Tails**: All return distributions exhibit excess kurtosis, indicating a higher probability of extreme events than predicted by the normal distribution. This has implications for Value-at-Risk calculations and tail risk management.

2. **Volatility Clustering**: The significant ARCH effects and high GARCH persistence parameters confirm that large price movements tend to be followed by large movements (of either sign). This is visually evident in the conditional volatility plots.

3. **Asymmetry**: Several commodities show significant skewness, suggesting that negative returns may be more extreme than positive ones, consistent with the leverage effect documented in equity markets.

### Implications for Portfolio Management

The heterogeneous volatility dynamics across commodities create opportunities for portfolio diversification:

- **Counter-cyclical volatility**: Some commodities exhibit volatility spikes at different times, suggesting potential for risk reduction through diversification [@markowitz1952].
- **Regime-dependent allocation**: The time-varying nature of conditional volatility supports dynamic allocation strategies that adjust portfolio weights based on current volatility estimates.
- **Risk budgeting**: GARCH-based volatility forecasts provide essential inputs for risk parity and risk budgeting portfolio construction approaches.

### Limitations

While GARCH(1,1) provides a robust baseline, several extensions could improve modeling accuracy:

- **Asymmetric models** (EGARCH, GJR-GARCH) to capture the leverage effect
- **Heavy-tailed innovations** (Student-t, GED) to better fit empirical distributions
- **Multivariate GARCH** (DCC-GARCH) to model time-varying correlations
- **Regime-switching GARCH** to capture structural breaks in volatility [@vivian2012commodity]

## Conclusions

This study provides comprehensive evidence of heteroskedastic behavior in agricultural commodity returns using GARCH(1,1) models. Key conclusions include:

1. All eight agricultural commodities exhibit significant volatility clustering and ARCH effects, validating the GARCH modeling framework.
2. Volatility persistence is uniformly high across commodities, indicating that shocks to volatility dissipate slowly.
3. Return distributions are consistently non-normal with fat tails, necessitating robust risk management approaches.
4. The heterogeneous volatility patterns across commodities support the case for diversified portfolio strategies.

These findings provide essential inputs for the subsequent stages of our research program, including multi-objective portfolio optimization and machine learning-based price forecasting.

Evolutionary Dynamics in Commodity Markets

An Agent-Based Simulation with Heterogeneous Agents and Replicator Dynamics

Author
Affiliations

Rodrigo Hermont Ozon

PUCPR — Graduate Program in Production and Systems Engineering

FAE Business School

Published

February 10, 2026

Abstract

We present an artificial commodity market model based on heterogeneous agents (ABM) where four trading strategies — fundamentalist, momentum, mean reversion, and noise — compete under replicator dynamics with mutation. Prices emerge endogenously from agent interactions. We demonstrate that (i) momentum agent dominance generates endogenous bubbles, (ii) selection intensity controls the transition between strategic diversity and winner-takes-all regimes, and (iii) polymorphic equilibria emerge when mutation rates are sufficiently high. An interactive swarm intelligence-style simulation is provided for result exploration.

Keywords

agent-based modeling, evolutionary game theory, replicator dynamics, commodity markets, bounded rationality

1 Introduction

Orthodox economic theory models financial markets under the assumption of representative agents with rational expectations. In this paradigm, prices reflect all available information and deviations from fundamental value are transitory (Fama 1970). However, the persistence of speculative bubbles, volatility clustering, and fat tails in return distributions systematically challenges these predictions (Mandelbrot 1963; Lux and Marchesi 1999).

Agent-based modeling (ABM) offers a constructive alternative. Rather than deriving analytical equilibria under perfect rationality, ABMs allow macroeconomic patterns to emerge from the interactions of heterogeneous agents with bounded rationality (Tesfatsion and Judd 2006). This approach is particularly fruitful when combined with evolutionary game theory, where natural selection of strategies replaces the hypothesis of individual optimization (Weibull 1995).

This work integrates three theoretical traditions:

  1. Heterogeneous agent models in the tradition of Brock and Hommes (1998), where fundamentalist and chartist traders coexist and generate complex endogenous dynamics.

  2. Evolutionary game theory with replicator dynamics (Taylor and Jonker 1978), where the proportion of each agent type evolves according to its relative market success.

  3. Complexity economics from the Santa Fe Institute (Arthur, Durlauf, and Lane 1997), recognizing that markets are complex adaptive systems.

2 Literature Review

2.1 Heterogeneous Agent Models

The seminal model of Brock and Hommes (1998) demonstrated that the interaction between fundamentalists and chartists is sufficient to generate complex dynamics replicating stylized facts of financial markets. Lux and Marchesi (1999) proposed a model where optimistic and pessimistic traders switch strategies through imitation, generating return distributions with heavy tails.

Hommes (2006) shows that expectation heterogeneity is a necessary ingredient to explain stylized facts such as volatility clustering, excess kurtosis, and long memory in returns.

2.2 Evolutionary Dynamics in Markets

The application of evolutionary dynamics to financial markets gained traction with Blume and Easley (1992) on portfolio rule selection. Evstigneev, Hens, and Schenk-Hoppé (2006) formalized an evolutionary theory where strategies compete for wealth as a scarce resource, in direct analogy with ecological competition.

Bowles (2004) integrated evolutionary games with institutional analysis, showing that norms and preferences co-evolve with the economic environment.

2.3 ABM in Commodities

Agricultural commodity markets present characteristics that make ABM particularly relevant: stochastic supply shocks, participation of hedgers and speculators with different horizons, and strong influence from geopolitical factors (Irwin and Sanders 2009). Ghoddusi, Creamer, and Rafizadeh (2019) document the growing application of computational techniques to commodity market analysis.

3 The Model

3.1 Agents and Strategies

The model defines four agent types, each representing an empirically observed decision heuristic.

Fundamentalist — Trades toward convergence with fundamental value: \(d_i^F(t) = \alpha \cdot \frac{P_t^* - P_t}{P_t^*}\), where \(\alpha\) is agent sensitivity.

Momentum (Trend-follower) — Extrapolates recent price trends: \(d_i^M(t) = \alpha \cdot \frac{P_t - P_{t-\tau}}{\tau \cdot P_{t-\tau}}\), with lookback window \(\tau = 5\).

Mean Reversion — Bets on price convergence to the moving average: \(d_i^R(t) = \alpha \cdot \frac{\bar{P}_{t,20} - P_t}{\bar{P}_{t,20}}\).

Noise Trader — Stochastic demand: \(d_i^N(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_N^2)\). Serves as a behavioral benchmark and uninformed liquidity provider.

3.2 Price Formation

The market price is determined by the population-weighted aggregate demand:

\[ P_{t+1} = P_t \cdot \left(1 + \sum_{s \in S} x_s(t) \cdot d_s(t) + \varepsilon_t \right), \quad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \tag{1}\]

where \(x_s(t)\) is the fraction of type-\(s\) agents, \(d_s(t)\) is the aggregate demand from type \(s\), and \(\varepsilon_t\) is a stochastic market shock.

The fundamental price follows a random walk with drift:

\[ P_t^* = P_{t-1}^* \cdot (1 + \mu + \eta_t), \quad \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_f^2) \tag{2}\]

3.3 Replicator Dynamics with Mutation

The proportion of each type evolves according to the modified replicator equation:

\[ x_s(t+1) = (1 - \mu) \cdot x_s(t) \cdot \frac{f(\pi_s(t))}{\sum_{j \in S} x_j(t) \cdot f(\pi_j(t))} + \frac{\mu}{|S|} \tag{3}\]

where \(f(\pi_s) = \exp(\beta \cdot \pi_s)\) is the fitness function, \(\beta\) is the selection intensity, \(\mu\) is the mutation rate, and \(|S| = 4\).

When \(\beta \to 0\), selection is neutral. When \(\beta \to \infty\), the system tends toward winner-takes-all. The mutation rate \(\mu\) prevents fixation and sustains diversity, generating polymorphic equilibria.

3.4 Wealth and Fitness

\[ W_s(t+1) = W_s(t) \cdot (1 + d_s(t) \cdot r_t) \tag{4}\]

where \(r_t = (P_{t+1} - P_t) / P_t\) is the market return.

5 Results and Discussion

5.1 Emergent Market Regimes

The model produces three qualitatively distinct regimes:

Regime 1 — Fundamentalist efficiency (low \(\beta\), low \(\sigma\)): Price tracks the fundamental. Fundamentalists dominate. This corresponds to the efficient markets hypothesis.

Regime 2 — Bubble-crash cycle (high \(\beta\), moderate \(\sigma\)): Momentum traders temporarily dominate, generating persistent deviations. When the bubble becomes extreme, fundamentalists regain market share, triggering an abrupt correction. Consistent with Brock and Hommes (1998).

Regime 3 — Polymorphic coexistence (high \(\mu\)): High mutation maintains all four strategies in fluctuating proportions. No strategy dominates in a sustained manner.

5.2 Implications for Portfolio Management

The optimal strategy composition is regime-dependent. In markets with clear trends (e.g., soybean rally after a crop failure), momentum generates alpha. In range-bound markets, mean reversion dominates.

Reflexivity is real: the mass adoption of a strategy alters the conditions that made it profitable. When everyone adopts momentum, the resulting bubble creates the conditions for its own destruction.

Strategic diversity functions as an implicit hedge, similar to the minimum variance portfolio of Markowitz (1952), but in strategy space rather than asset space.

5.3 Connections to Multi-Objective Optimization

The choice of parameters (\(\beta\), \(\mu\), \(\alpha\), \(\sigma\)) can be formalized as a multi-objective optimization problem where maximizing returns and minimizing bubble probability generates a Pareto frontier mapping the trade-off between efficiency and stability — connecting this work with multi-criteria decision-making frameworks (Reynoso-Meza et al. 2020).

6 Concluding Remarks

A model with four agent types and elementary heuristics, competing under replicator dynamics, generates phenomena that replicate empirical characteristics of commodity markets: endogenous bubbles, volatility clustering, regime switching, and strategy coexistence.

Future extensions: (i) social influence networks among agents, (ii) multiple commodities with cross-correlation, (iii) empirical calibration using B3 futures data, and (iv) multi-objective formulation for market regulation design.

## Introduction {#sec-intro}

In this paradigm, prices reflect all available information and deviations from fundamental value are transitory [@fama1970efficient]. However, the persistence of speculative bubbles, volatility clustering, and fat tails in return distributions systematically challenges these predictions [@mandelbrot1963variation; @lux1999scaling].

Agent-based modeling (ABM) offers a constructive alternative. Rather than deriving analytical equilibria under perfect rationality, ABMs allow macroeconomic patterns to *emerge* from the interactions of heterogeneous agents with bounded rationality [@tesfatsion2006handbook]. This approach is particularly fruitful when combined with evolutionary game theory, where natural selection of strategies replaces the hypothesis of individual optimization [@weibull1995evolutionary].

This work integrates three theoretical traditions:

1. **Heterogeneous agent models** in the tradition of @brockHommes1998, where fundamentalist and chartist traders coexist and generate complex endogenous dynamics.

2. **Evolutionary game theory** with replicator dynamics [@taylor1978evolutionary], where the proportion of each agent type evolves according to its relative market success.

3. **Complexity economics** from the Santa Fe Institute [@arthur1997economy], recognizing that markets are complex adaptive systems.
